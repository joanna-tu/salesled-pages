# First-call prep evals

Ten pattern-level scenarios for first-meeting preparation, the must-knows for each, and the core that repeats across all of them. That repeating core, with a real-or-faked test per item, lives in `09-the-reality-check.md`. This file is the derivation: the evidence that the standard was extracted from cases, not written in an armchair.

Every scenario is fictional at the company level and real at the pattern level. Three of them shadow live runs of the X-ray that were done by hand with verified sources (scenarios 2, 4 and 5); the traps in those three were found the expensive way.

How to use this file: each scenario lists what a seller must know walking into that meeting. Run a scenario through any AI research tool, then grade the output against the must-knows and the reality check. What the tool missed or faked is what a harness has to fix. That grading run is the next step of the plan; this file is the question paper.

## Scenario 1 - The listed company, cold first meeting

You sell commerce infrastructure. The target is a mid-cap listed retailer. You are meeting the chief digital officer. The meeting was booked cold; there is no history.

Must-knows:

- Their business model in two lines, and whether digital is their growth engine or their defense.
- Core revenue direction, and the online versus stores split, with a source and date on every number.
- The yardstick behind every read: against their past, their peers, or their stated goal.
- The risk factor closest to what you sell, quotable with document and year.
- Every dated event from the last 90 days that touches digital, and the timing tier they add up to.
- Postings under the CDO's org, seen live on the careers page during the run.
- The incumbent platform named with its detection method, how long it has been in, and how deeply it is wired in.
- What the CDO owns and the number they answer for, labeled as a guess with reasoning.
- Whether the CDO built the current stack or inherited it.
- The likely economic buyer and likely blockers, with one warm path or honest unknown each.
- Any figure where two sources disagree, both versions shown.
- The one stale or conflicting figure not to say out loud.
- The problem sentence an insider would nod at.

**The trap: the prepared-remarks brief.** Retelling the investor-deck narrative as if it were findings. The tell is smooth prose with no dates.

## Scenario 2 - The brand inside the group

The target is a well-known consumer brand that is legally one line inside a listed group. You are meeting the brand's managing director.

Must-knows:

- Which numbers exist at brand level and which only at group level, said explicitly.
- Group figures never dressed as brand figures; "consolidated" glossed in plain words.
- Where budget authority sits, brand or group, labeled as a guess.
- The group's visible strategy for this brand: invest, harvest, or quietly shop it.
- The sibling brands competing for the same group budget.
- The group risk factors that land on this brand's desk.
- Brand-level events from the last 90 days, kept separate from group-level noise.
- Postings for the brand versus group shared services, told apart.
- Whether the MD built the current setup.
- The warm path that runs through group functions, not just the brand.
- Number conflicts between the group filing and brand press coverage, both shown.
- The problem in the brand's words, not the group's.

**The trap: the altitude fake.** A brief that quietly answers brand questions with group numbers. The tell: no line saying which numbers do not exist at brand level.

## Scenario 3 - The VC-backed scale-up

The target is a private scale-up, two funding rounds in, no stock-exchange filings. You are meeting the VP who owns the function you sell into.

Must-knows:

- The national company registry for their country of registration, checked before the money is declared unreadable; much of Europe publishes private-company accounts, and the brief says which registry was checked and what it holds.
- The last round: date, size, lead investors, and the stated use of funds.
- A runway guess, labeled as exactly that.
- Founder and executive interviews as the substitute earnings call: what repeats across them.
- Strategy drift between older interviews and the last six months.
- Hiring as the loudest budget signal, verified live.
- The metric the company brags about, and the ones it never mentions.
- Confidence labeled honestly: most of the money section is hypothesis, and the brief says so upfront.
- The tech stack, read out of posting requirements.
- Who actually decides at this size, often the founder plus one.
- Events from the last 90 days: launches, partnerships, market entries.
- What was searched and not found; private companies produce many dry sweeps, and each is read.
- The problem statement in the vocabulary of their own blog and posts.

**The trap: false precision.** Financial confidence no source can support. The tell: revenue "facts" for a company that publishes no revenue.

## Scenario 4 - The founder who is the committee

The target is a founder-owned private company and you are meeting the founder. (Shadowed by a live run: a private media company where the committee map collapsed into one chair.)

Must-knows:

- The decision mechanics: this is a committee of one, and the meeting is the decision venue.
- The champion test inverted: there is nobody to sell internally; the real test is what the founder does between calls.
- That everything is authored: the founder built the current way, so every criticism of it lands on them personally.
- Their public story: origin, pride points, what they say no to.
- Money mechanics without filings: registry data where available, labeled by confidence.
- Speed: founder deals close faster and die faster; the timing rules change.
- The forwardable becomes a founder-facing summary they can hand their number two.
- Their number: the business itself is their number.
- Events from the last 90 days at their scale, not enterprise scale.
- What silence implies at a company that publishes little.
- Your own side of the table checked; small markets tangle.
- The problem statement in the founder's own vocabulary.

**The trap: committee theater.** A filled-in stakeholder table for seats that do not exist. The tell: roles with generic titles the company does not have.

## Scenario 5 - The fresh mandate

The person you are meeting took the seat within the last 90 days. (Shadowed by a live run that caught the stale-goal trap the hard way.)

Must-knows:

- Their start date, where they came from, and what the move says about the mandate.
- That every goal announced before their arrival carries the predecessor's name until confirmed.
- Their first public statements since taking the seat.
- Hiring opened under them since arrival, verified live.
- The window mechanics: the first two quarters are when a new owner rethinks how their area runs.
- The congratulations-with-substance opener this situation naturally offers.
- What they inherited versus what they are building; an arriver is an inheritor by definition, and that changes the pitch.
- Who they brought with them or promoted early.
- The number they were hired to move, labeled as a guess.
- The predecessor's fate, read as a politics signal.
- The watch-out: the predecessor's target quoted as the current plan.
- The stack: arrival plus hiring pointing the same way is a stack worth naming.

**The trap: the stale-goal brief.** Quoting the old regime's targets as the current agenda. The tell: goals dated before the person's start date presented as current.

## Scenario 6 - The account with history

HISTORY is given: your company lost an evaluation here two years ago.

Must-knows:

- Why the deal was lost, in whose words, with internal facts marked internal.
- Who from that evaluation still sits there, and who left.
- What changed since: leadership, stack, numbers.
- An opener that acknowledges the past; it must never pretend to be a first touch.
- What the loss taught about how this account decides.
- Current events read against the old context, not in a vacuum.
- Whether the winning vendor is still in place and how it is performing, from review echoes.
- The role your meeting contact played in the last evaluation, if any.
- The watch-out: the old pricing and scope discussion.
- The delta framing: what is honestly different this time.
- Committee changes since the loss.
- The problem statement updated to now, not recycled from the old cycle.

**The trap: the amnesia brief.** Researching as if you never met. The tell: HISTORY provided and never surfacing on the meeting card.

## Scenario 7 - The morning after the incident

The target had a public outage in exactly the area you solve, ten days ago.

Must-knows:

- The incident timeline, verified from primary coverage, not summaries of summaries.
- Their public response: who spoke, in what tone, promising what.
- Who owned the response internally; that person's desk is the meeting agenda.
- The cost echoes: reviews, social, press.
- Fix signals since: hiring, statements, visible vendor motion.
- The tone rule: helpful or silent; gloating reads instantly and is remembered.
- Never lead with the incident; lead with the cost of the current way.
- What the incident proves about the current stack, framed as their evidence, not yours.
- The risk factor that predicted it, if one was filed.
- The decision window: incidents unfreeze budgets fast and the window decays fast.
- The watch-out: implying blame of anyone who might be in the room.
- The problem in their words; their comms team has likely already written it.

**The trap: the ambulance-chaser brief.** The incident as sales trigger in the first line. The tell: an opener you could not say to their face.

## Scenario 8 - The cost-cutting quarter

The target announced layoffs three weeks ago.

Must-knows:

- The two-edged read, stated: a freeze for expansion tools, a window for efficiency tools, and which one you are.
- Which functions were cut and which protected; the protected list is the priority list published by accident.
- The stated reason for the cuts versus the reason in independent coverage.
- Timing decency: not in the first days; three weeks changes the math.
- The cost-of-the-current-way framing; never the news itself.
- What the cuts did to the team you sell into.
- Executive statements on what stays funded.
- Hiring still open despite the cuts, verified live; that is the loudest signal there is.
- Morale echoes in reviews, labeled as echoes.
- Momentum scored honestly: layoffs alone are context, not an act-now trigger.
- The watch-out: an efficiency pitch that sounds like "we help you cut more people".
- The problem statement in the CFO's vocabulary.

**The trap: the single-direction read.** Treating layoffs as a pure buying signal or a pure freeze. The tell: no sentence saying which edge applies to you and why.

## Scenario 9 - The tangled table

Your company's owner or group has interests that touch the target's world. (Shadowed by a live run where the seller's parent company turned out to compete with the target's core product.)

Must-knows:

- Your own ownership map: who owns your company, and what else they own.
- The target's ownership and partnerships that touch your rivals.
- The overlap named precisely: where their interests and your group's collide.
- Whether the conflict is disqualifying, manageable, or cosmetic, labeled as a guess with reasoning.
- How they likely see it; assume they already know, because small markets always know.
- Naming it first as the trust move; the meeting may be won on the disclosure.
- The deal-viability question asked early, before deep research is spent.
- Who on your side must clear the conflict.
- Events from the last 90 days on both sides of the tangle.
- The watch-out: discovering the conflict from their side of the table, in the room.
- Permission for the honest verdict: "do not pursue" is a pass, not a failure.
- A problem statement that survives the conflict being known.

**The trap: the one-sided brief.** Researching only their side of the table. The tell: your company appears only in the working assumption line.

## Scenario 10 - The quiet account

Perfect fit on paper, and nothing is happening.

Must-knows:

- The honest tier: nurture, said plainly.
- The listen-for list: the three or four events that would flip this account to act.
- The recommended move: a scheduled re-check, not outreach.
- Thin sections kept thin; no padding.
- Every dry sweep named, with what the silence implies.
- What the quiet says about how this company buys.
- The one long-term fact worth banking: a filed risk, a stated strategy.
- The fit documented for the day the account wakes.
- The delta groundwork: this profile is the baseline the next run diffs against.
- The watch-out: manufacturing urgency out of trivia.
- If the meeting happens anyway: its purpose is finding the trigger research cannot see from outside.
- A problem statement still drafted, marked as unpressured.

**The trap: padding.** Trivia inflated into momentum to justify the meeting. The tell: a momentum table where the Fresh column is full of no while the tier says act.

## The repeating core

Ten different rooms, and the same items keep coming back in different clothes: the swap test on the opening lines, sourced and dated numbers, yardsticks under verdicts, the self-written fear, the dated event or the honest none, live-verified postings, the person's number as a labeled guess, authorship, the mapped purchase with honest holes, surfaced conflicts between sources, reported silence, the one thing not to say, your own side of the table, the problem in their words, the incumbent with its detection method, and a label on every claim.

Those sixteen are the reality check. They live in `09-the-reality-check.md`, each with a one-sentence test that separates a real finding from a confident fake, plus the self-check the AI runs before delivering. What stays in this file is the situational layer: the twists and the traps that change shape from room to room.
